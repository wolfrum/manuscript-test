Brendan stood near a bench in the middle of the room and watched people, mostly arab with a few westerners, move through the room. Light streamed through windows into the Prehistoric Gallery of the Iraqi National museum. The stone walls and floor were cool and immaculate. Glass display cases neatly arranged, inviting visitors to examine artifacts dating back to the origins of human civilization.

 He walked over to a glass case holding three shelves of clay pottery. The placard dated the objects at 3000 BCE to 3500 BCE, and gave a brief summary of the civilization. Brendan skimmed the words as he contemplated the vast amount of time between now and when the clay pots were made. 

Although Brendan grew up in an Irish catholic family, he had not been to church since he left home. The people and places described in the bible were ancient history to him, yet these clay pots were made by people who lived three thousand years prior to that. In his cupboard at home were handmade bowls and plates made by a potter in Spokanne, Washington. He wondered if she used the same methods as the artisans who made the pots in the glass case in front of him.

A woman with kind brown eyes dressed in a tan pantsuit, white shirt, and black hijab stood next to him. Her presence calm and controlled. Her perfume pleasant, airy, and elegant.

She introduced herself as Nadia Al-Rashidi, curator of the Ancient Mesopotamian Collection.
Brendan exteneded his hand. "Brendan Murphy with The Continental."

She shook Brendan's hand with a firm grip, much stronger than he anticipated. "I understand you requested a tour of our museum. Is this for a story you are writing?"

There was no sense in dancing around it. "I'm doing research for a story about the stolen antiquities trade."

"Is there a specific time period or artifact you are interested in?"

"I don't have a concrete angle on the story yet."

"I see," Nadia replied. "I'll do my best to provide some context. Follow me."

Brendan followed Nadia down the corridor. She stopped at the display of a small figurine slightly longer in length than Brendan's hand.

"Are you familiar with the Samarra culture?"

"I am not," Brendan said.

Nadia nodded gracefully. "The Samarra culture existed between 6000 and 5000 BCE and are recognized as the pioneers of hydraulic engineering."

The term hydraulic engineering was not one that Brendan expected to be associated with an eight thousand year old civilization.

"Does that mean the people who made this figurine were the among the first people to discover crop irrigation?"

"The first culture that we currently know of, yes."

Brendan leaned in for a closer look at the figurine. "It has a slightly odd shape. Her hips are much wider than the rest of her body. And is she wearing jewelry?"

"Very astute observation," Nadai replied in the tone of a professor encouraging a student. "The wide hips represent fertility. There are two theories regarding the jewely. Either it does in fact represent jewelry or those are tattoos."

"Tattoos?"

"Possibly. You seem surprised."

"Tattoos are forbidden in Islam," Brendan said. "So I wasn't expecting to see them depicted on a figurine of a goddess."

"You are correct, tatoos are certainly haram in Islam." Nadia said. "The Samarra culture predates Islam by at least 5,000 years."

Brendan took a moment, embarssed at his own ignorance. "In America, we designate hundred year old buildings as historical landmarks."

"Are you familiar with the greek goddess Athena?" Nadia asked.

"Somewhat. The goddess of love and war?"

"Yes. This figurine depicts Inanna, the oldest known goddess of both love and war."

Brendan read the dates on the placard again. His understanding of human history was centered around Ancient Roman culture. Maybe it was the constant comparisons between the fall of the Roman Empire and the disarray of the United States or the emphasis on the sophistication of Roman engineering. Either way, it was implied that the Romans were responsible for the pillars of human knowledge and modern society was just improving on those ideas.

"I have a question about Ancient Rome, do you mind indulging me?" Brendan asked.

"Certainly, go ahead." 

"I'm trying to place the Samarra culture on a timeline. How does it relate to the Ancient Romans?"

"The Roman civilization thrived from around 900 BCE to around 150 AD, roughly 4,000 years after Samarra."

Brendan thanked Nadia and she reassured him that he was not the first Westerner to ask a similar question.

Brendan thought about the people who lived here, in this same desert landscape of modern Iraq, thousands of years ago, and crafted the objects now in glass cases. He had read extensively about the Iraqi culture, but only as far back as the origins of the Ba'ath Party in the 1950s. He remembered learning that the cradle of civilization was between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, but somehow the cultural impact of that fact escaped him.

Brendan followed Nadia through the gallery and into the next section of the museum. She stopped at a large vase at the end of the corridor. He recognized it as the famous Warka Vase that was stolen and returned during the chaos of the U.S. invasion of Iraq in 2003. The vase was stolen from the museum and subsequently recovered, but it had been broken in several pieces and had been meticulously restored by the Iraqi Museum staff. He said none of this to Nadia.

"This vase is the one of the oldest pieces of complex reliefs carved into pottery." Nadia said.

She went on to describe the scenes of agriculture, worship, and sheep herding depicted on the levels of the relief. Brendan asked again about the timeline of the culture that created the vase and the location. Nadia explained that the vase was found just twenty five miles northeast of Baghdad. 

"Any other questions about the Warka Vase?" Nadia asked, as if she expected another question.

"No," Brendan said. "It's a beautiful piece."

Nadia turned and continued through the gallery. Brendan followed, deep in his own thoughts. He had been in museums as a casual observer and even written an article about alleged tax fraud involving donors and the Getty Museum in Los Angeles. This visit was different. History naturally compresses time, individual people, and details. Although there was nothing special about the tour that  Nadia likely had given this same tour to countless visitors and diplomats, her descriptions of the artifacts and cultures triggered something in Brendan. For the first time he thought deeply about the artisans that made these objects and considered what their lives were like.

Nadia stopped at a locked door, leaned toward the card reader with the badge hanging around her neck and opened the door.

"Follow me," Nadia said.

They walked into a transitional gallery, which had the same layout as the public galleries, but dust had settled on the floor. Some display cases were missing and the walls were bare, no descriptive placards.

Nadia stopped at a display case that was half filled with cylinder seals. She explained to Brendan how people rolled these seals across clay to produce a signature for business transactions. They were also used as jewelry and artwork that could be worn as a necklace.

Brendan listened attentively and gently asked a question. "My understanding is that cylinder seals, being small and portable, are particulary vulernable to looters. The recovery rate on cylinder seals from the museum's study collection seems to be substantially lower than the overall numbers," he said. "Is that due to identification challenges is it a a market channel issue?"

Nadia turned her attention from the case and directly to Brendan. Her look was warm, not hostile. It was the look of someone taking a measurement.

She said nothing.

Brendan waited. He had interviewed enough people to know the difference between someone thinking about their answer and someone deciding whether to give him the answer. Nadia was deciding whether to give him the answer. He stayed silent, the choice was hers to make.

"Primarily, identification," Nadia replied. "The cylinder seals from our study collection were catalogued on index cards before the invasion and only about thirty percent were photographed. Without a photograph, proving that a specific seal was a part of the museum's collection there is no concrete evidence that the seal was stolen. There is only a provenance gap, and courts are not interested in provenance gaps."

Nadia focused the cylinder seals on the left side of the case, ignoring the empty space on the right.

"Market channels are also an issue, but secondary to identification," she said. "The cylinder seals are small, easily hidden inside any legitimate shipment of goods or even a suitcase. Buyers are sophisticated enough to understand that forged provenance documentation is a cost of aquisiton."

She turned back toward Brendan. "So, to answer your question, identification challenges make the market channel problem effectively unresolvable."

Nadia turned and walked down the gallery corridor, Brendan took another look at the half-empty display case of cylinder seals before following her.

Nadia stopped in the middle of the gallery. "What made you ask specifically about our study collection?"

Brendan answered honestly. "The Bogdanos report. The gap in the cylinder seal recovery numbers from the study collection stood out to me. Are you familiar with the report?"

"I am."

Brendan did not ask a follow up question.

Nadia looked forward to the end of the gallery and then calmly pivoted left and led Brendan through a non-descript door.

Inside the room were eight large desks, each having four tall stools with microscopes in front of them, two on each side of the desk, and a bright light overhead. An array of objects were being examined on the desks by staff wearing white lab coats and sterile gloves.

Despite the functional nature of the scientific equipment in the room, the design was aesthetically beautiful, it felt like Milan. The staff moved around the room deliberately, professionals moving methodically with purpose.

"This is the conservation laboratory," Nadia said. "It is where artifacts are examined and documented prior to display in the museum."

Brendan followed closely behind Nadia. He put his notebook behind his back and held it with both hands, careful not to touch anything as if he were walking through an expensive trinket shop on vacation.

He observed a young woman wearing magnifying lense glasses like a jeweler as she delicately dusted a clay pot with a small paintbrush.

A man in a white lab coat spoke to Nadia in Arabic. She followed him back to his work station and examined a piece of pottery under the microscope. Her eyes lifted out of the microscope and she smiled. The man enthusiastically wrote in his notebook.

Nadia walked back to Brendan.

"You wrote a piece on the Getty for the Atlantic magazine."

Brendan was shocked she had read his work.

Nadia continued. "The one about wealthy collectors donating stolen artwork and writing off the appraisals as tax deductions. You noted that the countries the objects came from were treated like a footnote in their own story."

Unsure of what direction her question was going, Brendan nodded and waited for her to continue.

"I sent that article to everyone in my department. How long have you been with The Continental?"

Brendan's throat tightened. The Getty story was his last article before he began working on the Nate Hollis book. That book, and the aftermath, had effectively ended his journalism career. Or at least crushed his credibility, personally and professionally.

Brendan collected himself with a deep intentional breath. "That article on the Getty donors was difficult. It turns out that wealthy people don't appreciate it when a spotlight gets put on their lack of human decency."

"The priviledged, no matter how they obtained their wealth, certainly seem to have a knack for justifying their exploitation of other people," Nadia said and motioned for Brendan to follow her toward an unoccupied desk with several artifacts on it.

Nadia explained that under the new process each new item received by the museum was photographed, indexed, and stored in a database. There was an ongoing effort to do the same for the thousands of artifacts already in the museum vault.

Brendan pointed to a to figurine on the exam table. "What is written on the index cards next to the artifacts?"

"Each artifact has a card with the Iraqi Museum's internal indexing number written at the top along with a short description of the item," Nadia said.

"What does the 'IM' before the numbers stand for?"

"Iraqi Museum, most museums have a similar prefix to identify which institution owns the item."

Brendan opened his notebook and wrote down 'IM Iraqi Museum' at the top of the next blank page.

Nadia continued to tell Brendan about the difficult, and often dangerous, process of excavating an archaelogical site. Her descriptions of security, looters, and unscrupulous fixers could have easily been used to describe drug traffickers instead of ancient artifacts.

He followed Nadia as they exited the laboratory and walked back to the museum entrance.
Nadia took out her business card. "Can I borrow your pen?"

She scribbled on the back of the card. "This is my cell number. If you have questions, this is the best way to get in touch with me."

Brendan thanked Nadia and carefully tucked the card into his notebook. She walked down the corridor toward the laboratory. Brendan walked toward the exit while texting Kareem that he was ready to leave. He looked back and saw Nadia and another museum staff member walking away from him.
