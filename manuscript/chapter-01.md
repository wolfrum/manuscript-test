He walked into the Shabandar cafe, ordered a lemon tea, and sat down at an empty table near the wall. The room held a low and subdued tone, broken only by four men in the corner, smoking cigarettes and arguing about politics in short bursts.

A man slid into the chair across from him without asking.

The man was Arab, casually dressed, with sharp eyes that assessed the room before settling on him. Crow’s feet scored a series of lines around his eyes and his black hair showed flecks of gray throughout. He placed him at roughly his own age, though he wouldn’t have been surprised if the man were a few years older.

"You're early," the Arab man said, his voice flat.

"Not as early as you." He closed the book and slid it off to the side of the table.
The man glanced at the cover, then back at him. "What do you think of the book so far?"
He raised an eyebrow. "I think we struggle to break away from our instincts."

"We?" The man asked.

"Humans. The collective we." He paused. "As individuals, people tend to be kind and generous. But in groups, we consistently commit terrible atrocities against one another and have a habit of ignoring warnings left for us by previous generations."

"Well said," the Arab man replied.

"Have you read For Whom the Bell Tolls?" he asked, nodding toward the book.

"Of course. The Spanish rebels have many familiar characteristics." The Arab man gently cleared his throat. "Therefore, send not to know for whom the bell tolls, it tolls for thee."

"Indeed it does." He smiled, imagining what John Donne would think about his poem being repeated four hundred years later in a Baghdad cafe.

"You look surprised," the Arab man asked.

"I am," he replied. "Most people recognize the name Ernest Hemingway. Only a few have read his work."

"I see," the Arab man nodded. "Are you familiar with the saying, Cairo writes, Beirut publishes, and Baghdad reads."

"No, it's new to me," he considered it for a moment. "But it rings true."

An older man, maybe early sixties, walked past their table and patted the Arab man on the shoulder. They exchanged a brief, friendly greeting before the older gentleman continued on his way out the cafe and into the street.

"He runs a restaurant in Mansour," the Arab man said. "He’s been there a long time. Very good quzi."

"What's it called?" he asked. "I haven't had lamb in a while."

The Arab man studied him, ignoring the question. "Why are you here?"

"Why am I here?" he repeated.

"Yes. Why are you here?" The man’s tone hardened.

"You contacted me. You said that you have information about stolen antiquities," he held his eyes on the man. "I'm a journalist. It's my job."

The Arab man nodded once. "And this is a job to you?"

He studied the man across from him, trying to assemble the pieces. The man carried himself with a casual confidence and wore plain clothes—blue jeans, a tan polo shirt, and leather sandals. His forearms and biceps were muscular in the way of a laborer, not a weightlifter. Up close, his features were distinct, yet he had a face that could easily be lost in a crowd.

"I apologize for the harshness of that question. I see you are struggling to judge me," the Arab man said. "Ask me some questions. You’re the journalist, right?"

He smiled. "Sure. Let's start with your name."

"Samir Hassan, I was born in Baghdad and have lived here through wars, sanctions, invasions, and civil wars."

"Brendan Murphy. I was born in Chicago."

Brendan reached across the table and shook Samir’s hand. It felt like grabbing a hunk of concrete.

"What do you do for work?" Brendan asked.

"I have done many things to survive. I am fortunate." Samir paused. "My uncle was a lineman for the state utility company. He encouraged me to become an electrical engineer. I studied at university and went on to work for the state utility company. This was after the war with Kuwait, during the sanctions, and before the American invasion. Do you know about this time in Iraq?"
"I was young, but I remember the invasion of Kuwait," Brendan said. "But I don't recall much about the sanctions."

Samir nodded. "The sanctions were more destructive than the war. The air strikes destroyed our infrastructure. The sanctions made it impossible to rebuild." He spoke evenly, without anger. "They forced everyone, except the Ba’ath leadership, to scramble for essentials. University professors sold family heirlooms and drove taxis to feed their children." He paused. "But worse than poverty, the sanctions stripped people of their pride. Saddam and the party leadership continued to build palaces while the country suffered."

"I'm sorry," Brendan said. "Did you work for the state utility company during that time?"

"I did. I worked there through the American invasion. Then I was fired. The Americans assumed I was a Ba'ath party member."

"Were you?"

"Of course," Samir said. "We all were. It was mandatory."

"What did you do after?"
"By that time I had established a side business installing diesel generators." He hesitated. "And liberating electricity."
Brendan nodded. "For a business, electricity is life."
Samir smiled. "Yes. You get the idea."
As their conversation settled into a rhythm, Brendan began to understand the risk Samir had taken by sitting across the table from him. Samir had survived decades of war and sectarian violence, yet he chose to speak with an American journalist. And he had chosen Shabandar cafe, a place where he was clearly known. The choice was deliberate.
"I tried to give this story to the journalist that came before you. He wasn’t interested," Samir said. "What happened to him?"

The question surprised Brendan. Thomas had not mentioned a source connected to antiquities theft in his turnover report. In fact, he had not mentioned any sources at all.
"His assignment in Baghdad ended," Brendan said. "He moved back to the States and is covering politics in New York."

Samir glanced toward the street. "You see the two men at the kebab stand?"

"Yes," Brendan said. "They were there when I walked into the cafe."

"They’re Iraqi intelligence officers," Samir said. "They’re following you. Nothing to be alarmed about."

"Are you sure?"

"The tall one. I installed a diesel generator at his house. I assume the other one is his partner."

Brendan let that settle for a moment. "How do you know they’re following me?"

Samir looked around the room and grinned. "I don't see any other foreigners."

"Fair enough. So what can you tell me about stolen antiquities?" Brendan asked.

Samir gestured toward the pen and notebook on the table. "May I?"

"Go ahead."

Samir wrote down an address. "Friday at 3pm. Meet me at that address." He got up from the table.
"Before you go. Why meet me here? Why not someplace more private?"

Samir smiled. "Because I don’t trade in secrets." He tapped his chest. "I wear my intentions right here. That’s how I’ve stayed alive this long." He stepped away from the table. "See you Friday."

Brendan watched him disappear into the street, then shifted his attention toward the two men talking beside the kebab stand.
